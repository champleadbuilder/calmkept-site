What should you put on an emergency contact card?

An emergency contact card holds the few details someone needs first, where they can see them at a glance: who lives in the household, who to call, each adult's medical basics, and the doctor and pharmacy numbers. On the back, it can say where the important documents, the main accounts and the digital access notes are kept. It's the short version of a family emergency binder, small enough for the fridge, a bag or a sitter's folder.

The front: what a helper needs first

  • Household. Each adult, the kids and the pets, the home address, and the home and mobile phone numbers.
  • Call first. Three people, in order.
  • An out-of-state contact. Someone outside the area whom everyone calls if local lines are down.
  • Medical, for each adult. Blood type, allergies, conditions and medications.
  • Who to call. The primary doctor, the pediatrician and the pharmacy, with their phone numbers.

Keep each entry short. A card is often read quickly, by someone who doesn't know your household, so a name and a number work better than a paragraph.

The back: where everything is

  • Important documents. Where the birth certificates, passports, marriage or divorce papers, will or trust, power of attorney or directive, property deeds and vehicle titles, and the last three years of tax returns are kept.
  • Financial anchors. The primary bank, where the credit cards are stored, investments and retirement accounts, the life insurance policy number, and the phone number of your lawyer or financial advisor.
  • Digital access. The password manager you use, device unlock notes, and how email recovery works.

Write locations, not contents. "Fireproof box, bedroom closet" is enough to send someone to the right place. Full account numbers, PINs and passwords don't belong on a card that other people may see.

Filling it in

  • Fill it in by hand, in pen.
  • Date it. Fill in the "last updated" date, so anyone holding a copy knows how current it is.
  • Say where the original lives. Fill in the "stored at" line, so every copy points back to it.
  • Copy it. Make one for each place it might be needed: the fridge, a wallet or bag, the car, a sitter's or grandparent's folder.
  • Tell your "call first" people that they're on it, and check their numbers with them.
  • Ask, don't guess. For medical details you're unsure of, such as a blood type or the exact name of a medication, ask the doctor or pharmacist rather than guessing. A blank is better than a wrong entry.

Keeping it current

Review the card once a year, and whenever something on it changes: a new doctor, a new medication, a new phone number, a move. When you update one copy, update them all. The "last updated" date makes an old copy easy to spot.

Privacy

The card holds personal information, including medical details. Keep copies where the people who'd need them can reach them. Think about who else sees a card on the fridge, and keep the back, with its document and account notes, somewhere less public if you'd rather.

When a card isn't enough

A card covers the essentials. When you want room for every account, every policy, each person's full medical page, your children's routines and your household plans, that's what a family emergency binder is for. You can start with the card and build the binder over time; the card's sections map onto the binder's.
