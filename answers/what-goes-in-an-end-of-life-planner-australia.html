What goes in an end of life planner in Australia?

An end of life planner is a written record of your wishes and the practical details behind them: the people you'd want contacted, what you want medically, where the money and the policies are, how your digital life is stored, and where each signed document is kept. It's the record someone you trust would reach for if they ever had to act for you. You fill it in at your own pace, and you change it whenever life changes.

About you, and the people close to you

  • About me pages for one or two people, with the basics someone would be asked for
  • People close to you, and a picture of who's in your life
  • The first calls: the people you'd want contacted first, the professionals who should hear (your lawyer, accountant, financial adviser and insurers), and a simple contact tree so one call leads to the next

What you want, medically

One page records your medical wishes in your own words: your views on life support, feeding tubes, resuscitation and pain management, and who you'd want making medical decisions for you if you couldn't. The planner says plainly that this page records your wishes and isn't an Advance Care Directive.

A second page records where the documents are kept:

  • Your Advance Care Directive
  • Any enduring guardianship or other substitute decision-maker appointment
  • A resuscitation plan or goals-of-care form
  • An organ donor registration
  • Your Medicare card and private health insurance card

What these documents are called, and how they work, varies by state and territory. Your GP or a lawyer can explain how they work where you live.

The money

  • Accounts and debts: bank accounts, credit cards, loans and debts.
  • Investments.
  • Superannuation: the fund, the account number, and the beneficiary nomination, including whether it's binding or non-binding and when it was last confirmed.
  • Taxes: who prepares them, and where past returns are kept.
  • Other assets: crypto and other digital assets, safes and cash reserves.
  • A cancel list: the regular payments and subscriptions someone would need to stop.

Policies and property

  • Policies: life insurance, with the insurer and policy number; health and disability; property and vehicle; and any other policies.
  • Property: real estate, vehicles, valuables and heirlooms, and any business interests.
  • A beneficiary map: who you've named on each account and policy, in one view.

Your digital life

  • How your passwords are stored: the password manager you use, who has emergency access, and where the recovery codes are kept. Not the passwords themselves.
  • Your accounts: email, social media, cloud storage and devices, and the photos and files you'd want kept.

Documents and legal planning

  • Where the originals are: your will, trust documents, enduring power of attorney, Advance Care Directive, certificates of title, vehicle registration papers, birth and marriage certificates, passports, Medicare card and Tax File Number records, and recent tax returns.
  • Your will: who drafted it, where the signed original is kept, and the executor you've named.
  • Enduring powers: your enduring power of attorney (financial) and enduring guardian, with the dates they were signed and where each original is kept.
  • Children: guardianship wishes, if you have children.

Legal documents, their names and the rules around them differ between states and territories. The planner records what you have and where it is. A lawyer confirms what you need and how it works where you live.

The people and animals in your care

  • Pets: each animal's vet, microchip number, food, routine and medications, and who you've asked to look after them.
  • Children: the details someone would need to care for them the way you do.

Letters and notes

The planner has letter pages:

  • a note to the person you trust to carry things through
  • letters to the people you love, including grandchildren
  • a page for what you stood for

They're optional, and there's no right order to write them in. Some people write a line or two now and come back to it later.

Keep it current

Review the planner whenever life changes: a marriage or separation, a new grandchild, a move, a new account, a changed wish. Note the date and what you updated. Then record where the planner is kept, and tell the person you trust where to find it.
