What goes in an important documents binder in the UK?

An important documents binder is a written record of what your household has and where each piece is kept: identity papers, accounts and pensions, insurance, property and vehicles, your digital life, and any legal documents such as a will or a Lasting Power of Attorney. It doesn't have to hold every original. It has to say where each one is, so the person who steps in for you knows where to look.

Start with where the originals are

The heart of the binder is one list of documents and their locations. In the UK edition, that list covers:

  • Birth, marriage and adoption certificates, and any decree absolute or final order
  • Citizenship or naturalisation papers, and passports
  • Your National Insurance number letter
  • Military service records or discharge papers, if you have them
  • Your will and any trust documents
  • A Lasting Power of Attorney, and any Advance Decision to Refuse Treatment
  • Title deeds, and vehicle logbooks (V5C)
  • Your last three years of tax returns, and any major contracts

Next to each one, write where it lives: a drawer at home, a safe, a bank, your solicitor's office. A second table records where you'd request a replacement if an original went missing, so nobody has to work that out from scratch.

The people

  • Who to call first. The people to reach first, in order, with phone numbers, email and address.
  • The professionals who know you. Your GP, dentist, solicitor, financial adviser, accountant or tax adviser, and your insurance contacts, with the firm and any account or policy number.
  • People to tell later. Friends and family who'd want to know, without the urgency of the first calls.

Money, in plain terms

Record where the money lives. Balances change, so round them or leave them out.

  • Current and savings accounts: the bank, the account type, the sort code and the last four digits of the account number.
  • Credit cards, loans and the mortgage: the lender, and how each one is paid.
  • Investments and pensions: ISAs and other investments; workplace, personal, SIPP and State Pension, with the provider and the beneficiary you've named.
  • Taxes: who prepares them, where past returns are kept, and your UTR if you file Self Assessment.
  • Everything else: recurring subscriptions, and any crypto or other digital assets.

The UK edition suggests writing only the last four digits of account and card numbers here. Full numbers can sit in a password manager or a sealed envelope.

Insurance, property and vehicles

  • Life, health and disability, home and vehicle policies, and any others, with the insurer, the policy number and who to call
  • Each property: how it's owned, where the title deeds are, the mortgage, council tax, and any service charge or managing agent
  • Each vehicle: the registration number, the registered keeper, any finance company, and where the logbook is kept
  • Valuables and heirlooms, and any business interests

Health and care documents

A short medical section sits beside the documents. It holds each person's medical information and current medications. It also has a page recording where the health documents are kept: your NHS number and any private medical insurance cards, a Lasting Power of Attorney for Health and Welfare, an advance decision or advance statement, and an organ donor registration if you have one.

Your digital life

  • Your password strategy: which password manager you use, and who knows where the master password is kept. Not the passwords themselves.
  • Your accounts: email, social media, cloud storage and devices, and any recurring digital bills.

Legal planning, as a record

This section records what exists, who is named and where the signed originals are kept:

  • Whether you have a will, who drafted it, and who your executors are
  • Any trust, and its trustees
  • Each Lasting Power of Attorney, with the attorneys and replacement attorneys
  • Guardianship wishes for children, if you have them

The names and forms of some of these documents differ between England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. A solicitor where you live confirms which ones apply to you and how they work. The binder records them. It doesn't create or replace any of them.

Keep it current, and tell someone

Review the binder once a year, and whenever something changes: a new account, a move, a new policy, a document moved somewhere else. Tell one or two people you trust that it exists and where it's kept. A record only helps if the right person can find it.
